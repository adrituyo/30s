# The 17 rules

Each rule has the reason behind it, how to check it, and what a fix looks like. The reasons matter more than
the wording: when a portfolio breaks a rule and still communicates, say so and pass it.

**Contents**

## First screen (0–5 seconds)

### 1. Say what you do, in one sentence
**Why.** The reviewer is sorting people into "relevant" and "not relevant". If the first sentence does not
say what you do, they sort you by guess, or not at all. "Multi-disciplinary designer", "creative",
"passionate about crafting experiences" say nothing a competitor could not also say.
**Check.** Read the first sentence a visitor sees. Could it be pasted onto another designer's site unchanged?
Does it name the kind of work and the kind of problem or client?
**Fail** when there is no sentence, only a name or a greeting ("Hi, I'm Sam 👋"), or only adjectives.
**Fix.** `[What you make] for [whom], [proof]`. For example: "Product designer for fintech apps. Eight years,
most recently lead on the savings flow at Ledger." Write the rewrite using facts from the page.

### 2. Show work on the first screen
**Why.** The claim in the headline needs evidence immediately, and designers are believed through images. A
first screen of only text, a big empty hero or a looping abstract animation makes the reviewer do the scroll
before they have a reason to.
**Check.** At desktop and phone size, without scrolling: is at least part of a real piece of work visible?
Judge the worse of the two sizes. **Weak** when work starts just below the fold, or one obvious gesture away on
a canvas or horizontal site, and the page makes that plain. **Fail** when work is a click away or the first
screen gives no sign that there is any.
**Fix.** Shorten the intro, or put the first project beside or directly under it.

### 3. Signal level and proof
**Why.** "Good" is not enough to shortlist; the reviewer needs to place you: junior or head of, agency or
in-house, known clients or student work. Specific, checkable facts do this faster than any adjective.
**Check.** In the first screen or just after: years, current role, named clients or employers, awards, numbers.
Are they specific ("Nike, Logitech") rather than generic ("global brands")?
For a leadership target, proof means scope: team size, since when, what shipped under them.
**Also here:** contradictions. A different number of years in two places, two job titles, facts on the about
page that the home page never mentions. Mark **Weak** for facts hidden on an inner page, **Fail** for facts
that contradict each other.
**Fix.** One line of the strongest three or four names or numbers, next to the headline. For someone starting
out, the equivalent is honesty about it plus the strongest single project.

## Scan (5–15 seconds)

### 4. Strongest, most relevant work first
**Why.** Most visitors see the first project, some the second, few the fifth. Chronological order or "latest
first" hands the best position to whatever happened to be last.
**Check.** Is the first project the one that best matches the target? Is something weak or off-target sitting
in the top three?
**Fix.** Name the order you would use and why. Personal experiments and old work go last, or leave.

### 5. Few projects, each one edited
**Why.** A portfolio is judged by its weakest piece, and length reads as lack of judgement. Four to eight
projects is plenty; one more good image beats three more average projects.
**Check.** Count projects. Look for near-duplicates, student work beside senior work, unrelated disciplines
mixed with the main offer, and undated or clearly old work standing in for recent work (for a senior target,
nothing from the last three years is a gap the reviewer will see).
**Weak** above about ten. **Fail** when it is an archive with no selection.
**Fix.** Say which to cut or move to a separate page (a side discipline, such as film for an art director, is
often better on its own URL that can be shared separately).

### 6. Readable at a glance
**Why.** Scanning means reading thumbnails and titles, not paragraphs. If a project cannot be understood from
its image and one line, it is skipped.
**Check.** Does each project show a title and a few words on what it is (client, type of work)? Are images
large enough to judge? Is anything revealed only on hover (which does not exist on a phone)?
**Fix.** Title plus a short tag line per project, always visible. Bigger images, fewer per row.

## One project (15–30 seconds)

### 7. Say what your part was
**Why.** Work is usually made by teams, and the reviewer knows it. Without a role they must assume the
smallest plausible one. Passing off team work, or unsolicited concepts for famous brands, as your own client
work is the fastest way to lose trust when it comes out in an interview.
**Check.** Two separate things for the first project. *Authorship:* role, and team or solo. *Relationship:*
client, employer, or personal concept. A single site-wide statement ("art director and author of every project
here") settles authorship for all projects, as long as it sits where a 30-second visitor reads it: the home
page or the project itself, not only the about page. It does not settle relationship.
**Weak** when authorship is clear and relationship is not, or when the statement exists only on an inner page.
**Fail** when a famous name appears with no word on the relationship and nothing on authorship either.
**Fix.** One line per project: `Role · team · year · client or "personal concept"`.

### 8. Show one decision
**Why.** Finished screens show taste; a decision shows judgement, which is what seniority is paid for. Long
process sections (personas, sticky notes, double diamonds) are the opposite: they show that a process was
followed, not that anything was decided.
**Check.** Does the project say, anywhere near the top, one thing the person chose and why? Is there process
material that could belong to any project?
**Not required** for purely visual disciplines, where a caption is enough: pass it there.
**Fix.** Two lines: "We had X. I chose Y because Z." Cut generic process.

### 9. Show the change
**Why.** The reviewer wants to know what was different because this person was there. Before and after, a
result, a number, or what shipped all answer that.
**Check.** Is there a before/after, a metric, a launch, a quote, an award, or at least "shipped / live at"?
**Pass** with any one of those on the first project. **Weak** when only final images are shown, or when
something shipped and the page does not say what happened next. **Fail** when the target is a product,
strategy or leadership role and no project says what shipped or what changed.
**Fix.** A before/after pair, or one sentence with the result. If there is no number, say what shipped.

## Exit

### 10. One clear ask, one way to reach you
**Why.** A reviewer who is convinced should not have to hunt. A visible email outperforms a contact form, and
a line that says what to write for tells them they are in the right place.
**Check.** Is an email or one contact link visible without searching, at the top or the end of the page? Is
there one ask, or five equal social icons?
**Fix.** A sentence and an address: "Have a brand to build from zero? Write to me: name@site.com".

### 11. Aimed at something
**Why.** A portfolio that tries to fit every job fits none. The target decides the headline, the order and
what is left out.
**Check.** After 30 seconds, could you say what this person should be hired for? Does the page mix offers
(designer, developer, photographer, DJ) with equal weight?
**Fix.** State the one thing to be hired for; move the rest to an about page or separate pages.

## Everything around it

### 12. Works on a phone
**Why.** Links get opened from chat, email and social apps, so a large share of first visits are on a phone.
**Check.** At phone width: no sideways scroll, text readable, nothing that depends on hover, tap targets
usable, zoom not blocked, video does not hijack the screen. (Whether work shows on the phone's first screen
belongs to rule 2.)
With only a first-screen screenshot, mark on that and add "(partly)". With nothing but a viewport tag, **Not
checked**.

### 13. Loads fast
**Why.** A slow first screen spends the 30 seconds on a blank page.
**Check.** From the snapshot: script weight (a megabyte of script before anything shows is slow on a phone),
image formats, lazy loading. A quick server response alone proves nothing. With a browser: whether the first
screen appears within about two seconds, and loaders or intro animations that delay content. Without a
browser, mark on what the snapshot shows and add "(partly)".
**Fix.** Modern image formats (AVIF or WebP) at the size they are shown, lazy loading below the first screen,
no preloader.

### 14. Shares well
**Why.** The portfolio travels as a link in Slack, LinkedIn and messages, and the preview card is seen before
the page is.
**Check.** From the snapshot: a title that says who and what (not "Home", "Portfolio" or the name alone), a
description, and a share image (`og:image`) that loads, near 1200×630. Everything about the preview card
belongs to this rule, not to rule 15.
**Fix.** Title as `Name — what you do`, a one-sentence description, a simple 1200×630 card.

### 15. Readable by machines
**Why.** Recruiters search, and more and more of them ask an AI assistant for names. Both read text and
structure, not pictures. Text baked into images, or a page that is empty until JavaScript runs, is invisible
to them.
**Check.** From the snapshot: real text in the HTML, a single `h1`, alt text on images, structured data
(`Person` or `ProfilePage`), an about page with plain facts, `sitemap.xml`. `llms.txt` is a bonus, not a
requirement.
**Fix.** Put the facts (name, role, city, clients, years, contact) as text on an about page, and add
structured data that matches it exactly.

### 16. No friction
**Why.** Every obstacle is a reason to close the tab.
**Check.** Passwords on the main projects, cookie walls, intro animations, custom cursors and scroll
hijacking, autoplaying sound, dead links, placeholder text and file names left as titles, duplicates, "coming
soon" projects, an out-of-date year, captions that complain about clients, an email that can only be copied
and never read.
**Fix.** Remove them. If work is under NDA, show what can be shown and say that the rest is available on
request.

### 17. Who you are and how to reach you, always within reach
**Why.** The moment a reviewer decides to write is not the moment the page planned for. It comes halfway down
the third project, or on a project page they landed on from a shared link. If the name and the contact are
only at the top and the bottom, acting on that decision costs a long scroll, and some of them will not make
it. Rule 10 asks that a way to reach you exists; this one asks that it never leaves.
**Check.** Scroll to the middle of the home page, and to the middle of one project page, at desktop and phone
size. Without scrolling, can you tell whose site this is, and reach the contact in one action? A header, side
panel or small bar that stays on screen with the name and an email or contact link does it.
**Pass** when the name and a way to contact are on screen at every point, on both sizes. **Weak** when it
holds on one size only (a fixed side panel on desktop that scrolls away on a phone is the common case), when
the name stays and the contact does not, or when the contact sits behind a menu that has to be opened.
**Fail** when the middle of the page shows neither, or when project pages have no contact at all.
This needs a browser or screenshots taken mid-page. With only the HTML, mark **Not checked**.
**Fix.** Keep one small element on screen: name, role, and the email. It can hide while the full contact
block is already in view, at the top and at the end. Keep it small: a bar that covers a fifth of a phone
screen trades this rule for rules 12 and 16.
**Watch for the opposite failure.** A floating name or contact element that sits on top of text, captions or
the page's own buttons is in the way, most often on a phone. Content may pass under it while scrolling;
nothing should be left unreadable or untappable behind it when the page stops, including at the very end.
Mark this rule on whether contact is in reach, and count the obstruction under rule 12.
